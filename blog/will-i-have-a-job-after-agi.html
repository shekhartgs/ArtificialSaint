Will I have a job after AGI?

October 2026 · 6 min read

Nobody knows for sure, and anyone who tells you otherwise is guessing. But we can look honestly at the arguments on both sides, and at what is within your control today.

It is one of the most anxious questions of our time. If machines become as capable as people at most thinking work, or more capable, what happens to the rest of us? Will there be jobs? Will there be income? Will there be purpose?

This article does not try to frighten you or comfort you. It tries to lay out what we actually know, what we do not, and what a sensible person can do while the future unfolds.

First, what is AGI?

AGI stands for artificial general intelligence. Loosely, it means an AI system that can do most intellectual tasks a capable human can do, across many fields, rather than being good at only one thing.

There is no single agreed definition, and there is no agreed test that would tell us the day it arrives. Some researchers think systems approaching this level could appear within a few years. Others think it is decades away, or that it will arrive gradually rather than as one dramatic moment. Serious, informed people disagree. That uncertainty is the first honest fact to hold on to.

What history tells us

Fear of machines taking jobs is very old. Weavers feared mechanical looms. Farm workers feared tractors. Clerks feared computers. In each case, many specific jobs did disappear, sometimes painfully. But over time, total employment kept growing, because new technologies created new industries, new products and new kinds of work that nobody had imagined.

A useful pattern: technology usually automates tasks, not whole jobs. When a job is a bundle of many tasks, machines take some of them, and the human role shifts toward the rest. Spreadsheets did not end accounting; they changed what accountants spend their day doing.

This is the strongest argument for optimism, and it has been right for a long time.

Why this time might be different

The counter-argument is also serious, and it deserves a fair hearing.

  • Breadth. Past machines replaced muscle or narrow routines. AGI, by definition, would be able to learn almost any cognitive task, including the new tasks that would normally become new jobs.
  • Speed. Earlier transitions unfolded over generations. Software can be copied and deployed worldwide in months, leaving less time for people and institutions to adjust.
  • Cost. If an AI system can do a task well enough for a fraction of the cost of a person, many employers will choose the machine, even if humans are slightly better.

If all three hold at once, the historical pattern might not repeat. That is not certain. But it is not a fringe view either.

Reasons humans may still work

Even in a world with very capable AI, there are reasons to expect plenty of human work, at least for a long time:

  • People want people. We prefer a human teacher, nurse, coach, priest, chef or performer in many situations, even when a machine could technically do the job.
  • Trust and accountability. Someone must sign off, take responsibility and be answerable when things go wrong. Laws, customers and regulators usually want that someone to be a person.
  • The physical world is hard. Robots are improving, but plumbing, caregiving, construction and repair in messy real environments remain difficult and expensive to automate.
  • New demand. When things become cheaper, people often want more of them, and entirely new needs appear: managing AI systems, checking their work, designing experiences, and jobs we cannot yet name.
  • Adoption is slow. Even when a technology exists, organisations take years to rebuild processes around it. Change usually arrives unevenly, not overnight.

Reasons to take the risk seriously

  • Entry-level roles, where people traditionally learn their trade, may shrink first, making it harder for young people to start careers.
  • Gains could flow mainly to those who own the AI systems, widening inequality unless societies choose to share them.
  • Even if new jobs appear, they may require different skills, live in different places, or pay differently from the jobs they replace.
  • Policies such as retraining programmes, tax changes or universal basic income are still debated, untested at scale, and may not be ready in time.

Three possible futures

Rather than one prediction, it helps to hold several scenarios in mind:

  1. Gradual shift. AI keeps improving, but adoption is slow and uneven. Most people keep working, with job content changing a lot. This resembles past technology waves.
  2. Sharp disruption. Capable AI spreads quickly. Many roles shrink within a few years, causing real hardship before new work and new policies catch up.
  3. Post-work abundance. AI produces so much value that paid work becomes optional for many people, supported by some form of shared income. The big question then is not money, but meaning.

Reasonable people assign different odds to each. Planning for more than one is wiser than betting everything on your favourite.

What is in your control

You cannot decide when AGI arrives. You can decide how prepared you are, whichever future comes.

  • Use AI now. People who understand AI's strengths and limits will be the ones trusted to work alongside it. Familiarity is protection.
  • Move toward judgment, relationships and responsibility. Tasks that involve trust, care, negotiation, leadership or accountability are harder to hand over.
  • Deepen your domain. Expertise in a real field, combined with AI skills, is more valuable than either alone.
  • Build financial resilience. Savings, low debt and more than one income source give you time to adapt if your field changes quickly.
  • Keep learning as a habit. The specific skills will change. The ability to learn new ones will not lose value.
  • Invest in health and people. Physical health, mental resilience and strong relationships help you through any transition, and they matter even more if work becomes less central.

The question beneath the question

When people ask "Will I have a job?", they are often asking two things at once. Will I have income? And will I have a reason to get up in the morning?

The first is an economic and political question. Societies will have to decide how the value created by AI is shared. Staying informed and taking part in that debate matters.

The second is a personal one, and it is older than any machine. Purpose has always come from growth, service and contribution, not only from a pay slip. Whatever happens to jobs, those remain available to every one of us.

So the honest answer is this: you may well have a job, possibly a very different one. You may need to change more than once. And whatever the machines can do, the work of becoming a good, capable and caring person will still be yours.

A machine can carry your burden. Only you can carry your purpose.
